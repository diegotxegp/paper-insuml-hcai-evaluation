\keywords{Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence, Heuristic Inspection, Human-Guided Machine Learning, Human-Computer Interaction, Automated Machine Learning}


\abstract{Heuristic inspection methods are a well-established family of expert-based evaluation techniques in Human-Computer Interaction, enabling the assessment of interactive systems without user participation. However, existing instruments are limited to classical usability criteria and do not incorporate Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence (HCAI) dimensions, a gap that becomes increasingly relevant as artificial intelligence components permeate interactive systems. This paper extends heuristic inspection methods to the domain of Human-Guided Machine Learning (HGML) by operationalizing the two-dimensional HCAI framework into a scored heuristic checklist --- the HCAI-HGML Compliance Matrix --- that yields quantifiable human control and automation scores across nine ML pipeline phases. The instrument follows the structure of established inspection checklists, enabling aggregated measurement and comparative analysis by evaluators without user participation. Its application is illustrated through a case study of InsuML, an HGML tool designed for users without AI expertise, revealing both its strengths and the specific gaps that future iterations should address.}

\section{\uppercase{Introduction}}
\label{sec:introduction}

The incorporation of \gls{ml} into non-technical professional domains poses 
a fundamental challenge: how can domain experts leverage the predictive 
potential of \gls{ml} without requiring specialized training in data science? 
\Gls{automl} tools ---software systems that automate the construction of 
\gls{ml} models--- have taken significant steps in this direction, but their 
predominant orientation toward optimizing predictive performance has relegated 
equally relevant aspects to the background, such as process transparency, the 
possibility of user intervention and the comprehensibility of results 
\cite{karmaker2021automl}.

In parallel, the paradigm of \gls{hcai} has gained relevance as a conceptual 
and evaluative framework for the design of intelligent systems that place the 
user at the center of the process, not as a mere recipient of results, but as 
an active participant with control and interpretation capabilities 
\cite{shneiderman2022human}. However, the practical application of \gls{hcai} 
criteria to concrete \gls{automl} tools remains scarce in the literature: 
while theoretical frameworks abound, few tools have been systematically 
assessed against human-centered design principles \cite{capel2023human}.

This scarcity, however, is not merely a matter of quantity. Even when 
\gls{hcai} criteria are applied, a more fundamental gap persists: existing expert-based evaluation instruments in \gls{hci} --- heuristic 
evaluation with severity rating and scored heuristic checklists --- are 
limited to classical usability criteria \cite{nielsen1994usability} and do not incorporate \gls{hcai} 
dimensions. As \gls{ai} components become more frequent in interactive systems, this limitation becomes increasingly 
significant: evaluating whether a system provides meaningful human 
control or achieves an appropriate level of automation falls outside the 
scope of any existing inspection instrument. Key questions remain 
unanswered: which pipeline phases should be examined? What criteria 
determine whether a decision genuinely belongs to the user or to the 
system? How can the resulting human-automation balance be characterized 
in a reproducible and comparable manner? Addressing these questions is a 
prerequisite for extending heuristic inspection methods into a principled 
\gls{hcai} evaluation instrument for \gls{hgml} tools. This gap is 
precisely what the present work seeks to address, responding to the call 
for operationalized \gls{hcai} evaluation frameworks identified in recent 
literature \cite{xu2026hcai}.

This work therefore makes two interrelated contributions. First, it 
extends heuristic inspection methods by operationalizing the 
two-dimensional \gls{hcai} framework into nine pipeline phases, explicit 
responsibility assignment criteria and a scored heuristic checklist --- 
the \gls{hcai}-\gls{hgml} Compliance Matrix --- that yields a 
quantifiable \gls{hcai} profile for any \gls{hgml} tool. Second, it 
illustrates the application of this instrument through a case study of 
\gls{insuml}, a \gls{hgml} tool designed for non-expert users.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: 
Section~\ref{sec:background} reviews the relevant background on 
\gls{automl}, \gls{hcai}, \gls{hgml} and heuristic inspection methods 
in \gls{hci}; Section~\ref{sec:hcai_inspection_instrument} presents the \gls{hcai}-\gls{hgml} 
inspection instrument; Section~\ref{sec:application} presents \gls{insuml} 
and its \gls{hcai} evaluation as a case study; Section~\ref{sec:discussion} 
discusses the findings and their implications; and Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} 
summarizes the conclusions and future work.


\section{\uppercase{Background}}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{AutoML: Automation of the ML Pipeline}

\Gls{automl} tools emerged in response to the growing demand for \gls{ml} solutions and the shortage of qualified data scientists \cite{he2021automl}. Their purpose is to democratize access to \gls{ml} by automating complex tasks such as feature engineering, model selection, hyperparameter optimization and evaluation, making advanced analytics accessible to users without deep technical knowledge.

However, this predominantly automation-oriented approach frequently operates as a black box, making it difficult for users to understand decision-making processes, incorporate domain-specific knowledge or maintain meaningful control over the modeling process \cite{garouani2023unlocking}. This limitation is not minor: the lack of transparency can conceal underlying biases and prevent the human-AI collaboration necessary to obtain reliable and contextually appropriate results \cite{komala2024fair}. Recent proposals have attempted to address this limitation by integrating \gls{llm} into \gls{automl} pipelines, enabling users to interact with the modeling process through natural language \cite{miguel2025integrating} and receive intelligible explanations of automated decisions \cite{francois2025interactive}. While these approaches represent a step toward greater transparency and accessibility, they do not provide a structured evaluation framework under \gls{hcai} criteria, nor do they offer the guided, phase-by-phase pipeline control that characterizes \gls{hgml} tools.

The current market offers both commercial solutions ---such as Google AutoML, Azure Automated ML or Amazon SageMaker Autopilot--- and free alternatives ---including Ludwig, AutoGluon, H2O-3 or PyCaret---, designed to meet varying levels of technical expertise. However, the analysis of these tools reveals that none simultaneously maximizes human control and automation, each representing a different balance between accessibility, interpretability and technical sophistication \cite{palazuelos2024non}.

\subsection{Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence}

\Gls{hcai} represents a fundamental reorientation in the design and evaluation of intelligent systems. Unlike traditional approaches that prioritize algorithmic performance as the primary success criterion, \gls{hcai} places the user at the center of the system lifecycle, emphasizing that \gls{ai} should amplify and enhance human capabilities rather than replace them \cite{shneiderman2022human}. In the so-called third wave of \gls{ai}, \gls{hci} professionals play a central role not only in designing user experiences but also in shaping 
intelligent systems that are ethical, comprehensible and useful 
\cite{xu2019toward}.

A distinctive aspect of this paradigm is its two-dimensional conceptualization (see Figure~\ref{fig:HCAI_dimensions}). Unlike the classical notion of autonomy, which assumes an inverse relationship between automation and human control, \gls{hcai} treats both dimensions as independent. This allows conceiving systems that simultaneously combine high levels of automation with meaningful human control, representing the most desirable design space for \gls{ai} tools aimed at non-expert users.

This duality is articulated around three human control criteria ---ease of use, pipeline configurability and information interpretability--- and six automation criteria ---data type detection, data cleaning, feature engineering, model training, hyperparameter optimization and model validation---, forming an evaluative framework applicable to concrete tools. Among the risks that \gls{hcai} seeks to mitigate are algorithmic bias, frequently caused by imbalanced training data, and the lack of transparency inherent to systems that operate as black boxes \cite{chen2023human}.


\subsection{Human-Guided Machine Learning}

\Gls{hgml} constitutes the practical realization of \gls{hcai} principles within the \gls{ml} domain \cite{gil2019towards}. Its fundamental premise is that the active participation of domain experts throughout the \gls{ml} pipeline is not only possible but beneficial, enriching the process with contextual knowledge that algorithms alone cannot incorporate. While \gls{automl} efficiently searches for solutions, expert input through intuitive interfaces enhances the process, resulting in more accurate, efficient and interpretable models \cite{lindauer2024position}.

Within the \gls{hgml} framework, this participation is structured around three main areas. In the data preparation phase, users can intervene in variable selection and transformation, the definition of subpopulations of interest and the management of outliers. During model development, the expert can guide the selection of algorithm families, impose constraints on parameters and influence pipeline design. Finally, in the results interpretation phase, users can request specific metrics, compare alternative configurations and analyze model sensitivity to parameter variations.

Despite its potential, existing \gls{hgml} proposals present notable limitations in practice. Although some theoretical frameworks cover specific parts of the pipeline, few offer complete software implementations accessible to users without \gls{ai} training, considerably reducing their applicability in real-world contexts \cite{gil2019towards,francia2023hamlet,lee2020human,lindauer2024position}.

\subsection{Heuristic Inspection Methods in HCI}

Heuristic inspection methods constitute a well-established family of 
expert-based evaluation techniques in \gls{hci} \cite{nielsen1994usability}. 
Unlike empirical methods that require the participation of representative 
users, inspection methods rely on evaluators --- typically between three and 
five domain experts --- who analyze an interface directly against a set of 
predefined usability principles. This makes them particularly suitable for 
early-stage evaluation, iterative design cycles and contexts where user 
recruitment is impractical \cite{lazar2017research}.

The \emph{heuristic 
evaluation with severity rating} asks auditors 
to identify usability problems and classify each according to its severity on 
an ordinal scale, enabling prioritization of redesign efforts based on their 
impact on the user experience. The \emph{scored heuristic checklist} 
transforms usability principles into a list of evaluable criteria, each 
scored quantitatively --- typically on a Likert-type scale --- yielding 
aggregate measures of the degree to which an interface complies with each 
heuristic. This approach facilitates comparative analysis and the presentation 
of results in numerical terms.

Despite their widespread adoption, existing heuristic inspection instruments 
are limited to classical usability criteria --- such as visibility of system 
status, user control and freedom, or consistency and standards --- and do not 
incorporate \gls{hcai} dimensions. This limitation is increasingly significant 
as \gls{ai} components become more frequent in interactive systems: evaluating 
whether a system provides meaningful human control or achieves an appropriate 
level of automation falls outside the scope of any existing inspection 
instrument.


\section{\uppercase{A HCAI Inspection Instrument for HGML Tools}}
\label{sec:hcai_inspection_instrument}

The inspection instrument proposed here adopts the structure of the scored heuristic checklist paradigm established in \gls{hci} \cite{nielsen1994usability}, but replaces its classical usability criteria with \gls{hgml}-specific dimensions of human control and automation that existing inspection instruments do not address. It operationalizes the two-dimensional \gls{hcai} framework \cite{shneiderman2022human} into observable criteria assessed across the nine-phase \gls{hgml} pipeline model \cite{gil2019towards}. Like established heuristic checklists, the instrument can be applied without user participation and produces quantitative scores that support aggregated measurement and comparative analysis, positioning a tool within the \gls{hcai} quadrant space and providing a phase-level diagnostic profile to identify gaps and guide targeted design recommendations.

\subsection{Dimensions and Criteria}
\label{subsec:dimensions}

The \gls{hcai} framework positions tools along two independent axes: human
control and computer automation. To make these
axes actionable for evaluation, each must be decomposed into observable
criteria that can be assessed by examining the tool's interface and behavior
across pipeline phases.

\emph{Human control} is operationalized through three criteria that capture
the user's capacity to understand, configure, and verify the system's behavior
at each stage of the pipeline. These criteria correspond to the conditions
identified by Shneiderman for meaningful human control in \gls{ai} systems:
that the system is usable without specialized expertise, allows users to shape
its decisions, and communicates those decisions intelligibly.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Ease of use}: whether the tool can be operated without
    \gls{ai} expertise, based on its interface design, interaction patterns,
    and absence of programming requirements.
    \item \emph{Pipeline configurability}: the extent to which users can
    customize key decisions across pipeline phases without code-level
    intervention, enabling the injection of domain knowledge.
    \item \emph{Information interpretability}: whether the system
    communicates its decisions, configurations, and results in formats
    accessible to non-technical users, enabling informed judgment rather
    than blind acceptance.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Computer automation} captures the degree to which the system
autonomously executes the technical tasks of each pipeline phase. It is
decomposed into six criteria reflecting distinct aspects of this
automated processing:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Data type detection}: whether the system identifies and
    handles the data types present in the dataset without explicit user
    specification.
    \item \emph{Data cleaning}: whether the system detects and resolves
    data quality issues such as missing values, duplicates, or inconsistent
    formats.
    \item \emph{Feature engineering}: whether the system generates,
    transforms, or selects features from the raw input data.
    \item \emph{Model training}: whether the system executes the training
    process, including architecture instantiation and optimization, without
    manual intervention.
    \item \emph{Hyperparameter tuning}: whether the system searches for
    optimal hyperparameter configurations within the defined space.
    \item \emph{Model validation}: whether the system applies validation
    strategies such as cross-validation or hold-out evaluation and reports
    performance estimates.
\end{itemize}

principled description of what each phase should look like in a fully
\gls{hcai}-compliant tool, specifying which decisions are expected from the
user and which from the system. Table~\ref{tab:balance} serves as the benchmark against
which any tool under evaluation is contrasted when populating the matrix.

\subsection{The HCAI-HGML Compliance Matrix}
\label{subsec:matrix}

The \gls{hcai}-\gls{hgml} Compliance Matrix is the central instrument of the
evaluation. Following the structure of scored heuristic checklists, it crosses 
the nine pipeline phases (rows) against four evaluation columns: the three 
human control criteria (\emph{ease of use}, \emph{pipeline 
configurability}, \emph{information interpretability}) and a single 
automation score ($A_{\text{global}}$) per phase. This asymmetric structure 
reflects a deliberate methodological choice: while the six automation 
criteria defined in Section~\ref{subsec:dimensions} provide a complete 
picture of a system's automated capabilities, not all criteria are present 
or relevant in every pipeline phase. Collapsing them into a single 
$A_{\text{global}}$ per phase avoids artificially penalizing tools for the 
absence of criteria that are structurally inapplicable to a given phase, 
while still capturing the overall degree of automation at each stage. Each
cell represents the degree to which the tool satisfies a given criterion in a
given phase, assessed by contrasting the tool's observed behavior against the
theoretical expectation defined in Table~\ref{tab:balance}.

All four criteria are scored on a five-level ordinal scale that provides
sufficient granularity to capture meaningful differences in implementation
quality without demanding a degree of precision that an interface
analysis cannot reliably support:
\emph{0.00}~(Absent --- the criterion is not implemented);
\emph{0.25}~(Minimal --- a rudimentary attempt with severe limitations);
\emph{0.50}~(Partial --- approximately half of the expected behavior is
present); \emph{0.75}~(Substantial --- the criterion is largely met with
minor identifiable gaps); and \emph{1.00}~(Complete --- the criterion is
fully satisfied).

Once the matrix is fully populated, two aggregate scores are computed. The
\emph{Human Control Score} ($S_{HC}$) is the mean of all human control
criterion scores across all phases:

\begin{equation}
    S_{HC} = \frac{1}{n \cdot c_{HC}} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j=1}^{c_{HC}} v_{ij}
    \label{eq:shc}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $n = 9$ is the number of pipeline phases, $c_{HC} = 3$ the
number of human control criteria, and $v_{ij} \in \{0, 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 1\}$
is the ordinal score assigned to cell $(i, j)$. The \emph{Automation Score}
($S_{A}$) is the mean of all $A_{\text{global}}$ scores across phases:

\begin{equation}
    S_{A} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} A_{\text{global},\text{i}}
    \label{eq:sa}
\end{equation}

Both scores range from 0 to 1 and together determine the \gls{hcai} quadrant
of the tool under evaluation, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:quadrants}. The
quadrant classification provides a coarse-grained summary of the tool's
\gls{hcai} profile; the distance metric introduced below refines this
characterization into a continuous measure.

\begin{table*}[ht]
\caption{\gls{hcai} quadrant classification based on $S_{HC}$ and $S_{A}$.}
\label{tab:quadrants}

\begin{tabular}{lllp{6cm}}

The \emph{distance to the ideal point} $(1, 1)$ provides a continuous
measure of proximity to a fully \gls{hcai}-compliant \gls{hgml} tool:

\begin{equation}
    D = \sqrt{(1 - S_{HC})^2 + (1 - S_{A})^2}
    \label{eq:distance}
\end{equation}

\noindent $D$ ranges from $0$ (perfect compliance) to $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$
(worst case). A tool in the optimal quadrant with a low $D$ value is close to
the ideal; a high $D$ value, even within the optimal quadrant, signals that
one or both dimensions require improvement. The column scores for each human 
control criterion and the row scores for each pipeline phase further localize 
where the primary gaps lie --- whether in a specific dimension of human 
control, in a specific stage of the pipeline, or in both --- enabling targeted 
design recommendations rather than a single summary judgment.


\section{\uppercase{Case Study: Application to InsuML}}
\label{sec:application}

This section illustrates the application of the inspection instrument through a case study of \gls{insuml}, an 
\gls{hgml} tool designed for non-expert users. We first describe the tool's 
architecture and interaction model, and then present the results of its 
\gls{hcai} inspection through the Compliance Matrix.

\subsection{Tool Overview and Architecture}
\label{subsec:tool_overview}

\Gls{insuml} is a software application, under active development, designed 
to facilitate the construction of predictive models by users without \gls{ai} 
expertise. Its design rests on a central premise: that domain knowledge and 
expert intuition are valuable assets that must be explicitly integrated into 
the \gls{ml} process, rather than being displaced by automation. To this end, 
the tool adopts the \gls{hgml} paradigm as its structuring principle, 
combining the power of an \gls{automl} engine with a guided \gls{gui} that 
makes every pipeline decision visible and controllable.

The interface is organized through a tab-based navigation system that guides users sequentially through the complete \gls{ml} pipeline without requiring programming skills. Navigation is bidirectional: users may return to any previously completed tab to revise their choices, but forward progression requires completing the current tab, ensuring that no pipeline phase is skipped inadvertently. Each tab exposes only the decisions relevant to its corresponding phase, preventing cognitive overload without sacrificing configurability. A distinctive feature of the workflow is a pre-training configuration summary presented immediately before execution begins: the system displays the complete set of user-selected options alongside the defaults applied automatically, allowing the user to review, correct or confirm every decision before committing to training. The feature configuration tab, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:tab-based_interface}, illustrates this approach: users can inspect the loaded dataset, assign variable roles and define missing value handling strategies directly from the interface, without any programming intervention. Once training is complete, results are presented through interactive graphics and confusion matrices that facilitate interpretation without requiring advanced statistical expertise.

\Gls{insuml} is built on three modular layers: an Interface Layer  
implemented in Python using Tkinter, which manages user interaction and  
input validation; a Core Logic Layer that translates user selections into  
technical configurations and automatically generates the necessary  
configuration files; and an \gls{automl} Layer based on the Ludwig  
engine, which handles training, hyperparameter optimization and model  
evaluation autonomously. This separation ensures that all automated  
decisions can be traced and understood, contributing to bias mitigation  
and responsible \gls{ai} deployment \cite{chen2023human}. The modular  
design also allows the underlying \gls{automl} engine to be replaced  
without altering the user experience, ensuring flexibility and longevity.

\emph{Ease of use} reaches a score of 1.00 across most phases, reflecting
the consistent absence of programming requirements and the progressive
disclosure of options through tab-based navigation. Three phases constitute
exceptions: model selection and parameter comparison score 0.00, reflecting
the complete absence of user-facing controls for these pipeline steps in the
current version of \gls{insuml}; and solution design scores 0.50, as the
controls available at this phase are operable without technical expertise but
are limited to preprocessing, leaving feature generation, imputation and
pipeline design inaccessible from the interface. The uniformity across the
remaining six phases reflects the decision to expose only the decisions
relevant to each phase, preventing cognitive overload.

\emph{Pipeline configurability} presents the most significant variation
across phases, generating the lowest aggregate score among the three human
control criteria (0.50). Variable requirements, instance selection and model
assessment score 1.00: users can specify inclusion and exclusion rules for
variables based on domain knowledge, define subpopulations and outlier
handling strategies, and select the evaluation metrics to be reported,
respectively. Parameter settings reaches 0.25, as users can specify execution
time and the number of candidate models to explore, but the remaining training
parameters are handled automatically by the engine. Variable selection scores
0.50: users can assign variable roles and define missing value strategies but
cannot prioritize variables or propose new feature combinations. Solution
design reaches 0.50, as internal pipeline construction choices ---such as
encoder selection or combiner configuration--- are not directly accessible to
the user; model comparison scores 0.25, as users can request model comparisons
but all comparison parameters and presentation are handled automatically by
the engine; and model selection and parameter comparison score 0.00, as
neither phase offers any user-facing intervention point in the current version
of the tool.

\emph{Information interpretability} is high across most phases
(0.75--1.00), supported by interactive visualizations, accessible language
and the pre-training configuration summary. Variable requirements, instance
selection, model assessment and model comparison all reach 1.00, as the
system communicates its decisions and results in formats that require no
statistical expertise to interpret. Variable selection, parameter settings
and solution design score 0.75, reflecting minor gaps in the completeness
or accessibility of the information provided at those stages. Model selection
and parameter comparison score 0.00: the former due to the complete opacity
of automatic architecture selection, a limitation directly attributable to
the underlying \gls{automl} engine; the latter because the phase is not
implemented in the current version of \gls{insuml}.

Across the automation dimension, the Ludwig engine achieves complete
automation ($A_{\text{global}} = 1.00$) in seven of the nine pipeline
phases: variable requirements, instance selection, model selection,
parameter settings, solution design, model assessment and model comparison.
Variable selection scores 0.75, reflecting substantial but not exhaustive
automation where certain preprocessing decisions ---such as outlier handling
or custom feature transformations--- remain outside the engine's default
scope and require explicit user specification. Parameter comparison scores
0.00, as this phase is not implemented in the current version of
\gls{insuml}.

Applying Equations~\ref{eq:shc} and~\ref{eq:sa}, the aggregate scores
are $S_{HC} = 0.64$ and $S_{A} = 0.86$, placing \gls{insuml} firmly in
the \emph{Optimal quadrant} of the \gls{hcai} framework, with a distance
to the ideal point of $D = 0.39$. The high automation score reflects the
Ludwig engine's broad coverage of pipeline tasks, while the human control
score reveals that configurability ---particularly during model development
phases--- and the absence of parameter comparison functionality remain the
primary dimensions for improvement.


\section{\uppercase{Discussion}}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results of the \gls{hcai} inspection of \gls{insuml} invite    
reflection on three levels: the positioning of the tool within the    
broader \gls{hgml} and \gls{automl} landscape, the design implications    
that the inspection surfaces for \gls{hci} practitioners, and the    
limitations of the inspection instrument and the tool that future work    
should address.

\subsection{InsuML in the HGML Landscape}

The \gls{hcai} inspection situates \gls{insuml} within the current
landscape of \gls{hgml} and \gls{automl} tools beyond predictive
performance. The frameworks of Gil~et~al.~\cite{gil2019towards} and
Lee~et~al.~\cite{lee2020human} offer comprehensive theoretical coverage
of the \gls{ml} pipeline from a human-centered perspective, but lack
operational implementations and have not been evaluated under \gls{hcai}
criteria. The HAMLET framework of Francia~et~al.~\cite{francia2023hamlet}
provides partial software support but targets \gls{ai} experts rather
than non-technical users. Lindauer~et~al.~\cite{lindauer2024position}
call for more human-centered \gls{automl} tools, acknowledging that
current proposals insufficiently address usability, interpretability and
customization --- criteria that are the primary success axis of \gls{insuml}.

To the best of our knowledge, \gls{insuml} is the only proposal combining
full pipeline coverage, operational software, accessibility for
non-\gls{ai} users and explicit evaluation under \gls{hcai} criteria.
Xu~et~al.~\cite{xu2026hcai} address \gls{hcai} conceptually; the present
work complements their contribution by grounding these principles in a
concrete, pipeline-specific inspection instrument applicable to any
\gls{hgml} tool.

\subsection{Implications for HCI-Oriented ML Interface Design}

Beyond the analysis of \gls{insuml}, the inspection instrument provides
transferable insights for designing \gls{ml} interfaces for non-expert users.
Three design decisions prove particularly consequential and are articulated
here as reusable \gls{hci} principles.

First, \emph{sequential tab-based navigation} structures interaction as a
guided progression through pipeline phases, exposing only decisions relevant
to each step, with bidirectional access to revisit and revise earlier choices.
This reduces cognitive load without sacrificing configurability, and its
consistent ease-of-use scores of 1.00 across most phases confirm it as a
reliable model for complex, multi-step \gls{ml} workflows.

Second, \emph{explicit responsibility allocation} --- making visible which
decisions belong to the user and which are automated --- directly supports
interpretability. The pre-training configuration summary exemplifies this:
it exposes every pending automated decision as an inspectable checkpoint,
allowing the user to review and correct the system's choices before training.

Third, \emph{modular architectural separation} between the interface,
core logic and \gls{automl} layers carries implications beyond maintainability.
Keeping the translation between user intentions and technical parameters
traceable decouples the user experience from the underlying engine, allowing
the \gls{automl} backend to evolve without disrupting the interaction model.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

The application presented in this work is not without limitations. Compliance Matrix scores were assigned by the tool's own authors, which may
compromise objectivity. Reliability studies involving three to five
independent domain experts would strengthen reproducibility and constitute
a natural step toward consolidating the instrument for general \gls{hgml}
evaluation. Results should therefore be interpreted as illustrative rather
than as evidence of formal validity.

The work also lacks empirical usability studies with domain specialists
performing realistic tasks and measuring completion time, error rates and
satisfaction. Such studies, following established \gls{hci}
methods~\cite{lazar2017research}, would complement the structural inspection
and provide evidence on whether identified design decisions yield measurable
improvements in user performance.

Finally, \gls{insuml}'s explainability is currently limited to global
performance metrics and confusion matrices. Incorporating \gls{xai}
techniques such as \gls{shap} or \gls{lime}~\cite{salih2025perspective}
would enrich model interpretation, enabling users to understand which
variables drive individual predictions. The absence of parameter comparison
further limits systematic experimentation; implementing it constitutes a
concrete priority alongside \gls{xai} integration. Extending the tool to
larger datasets and heterogeneous sources --- text, images, time series ---
would broaden real-world applicability while preserving its human-centered
design principles.
  
  
\section{\uppercase{Conclusions}}  
\label{sec:conclusions}  
  
This paper has presented two interrelated contributions: an extension of    
heuristic inspection methods to the domain of \gls{hgml} tools under    
\gls{hcai} criteria, formalized as an \gls{hcai}-\gls{hgml} Compliance    
Matrix with aggregate scores $S_{HC}$, $S_{A}$ and distance metric $D$;    
and a case study applying this instrument to \gls{insuml}, obtaining    
$S_{HC} = 0.64$, $S_{A} = 0.86$ and $D = 0.39$, and showing that    
high automation and meaningful human control are not competing objectives    
when interaction design places the non-expert user at the center.  
  
The case study demonstrates that \gls{insuml} achieves a genuine balance  
between both \gls{hcai} dimensions through deliberate design decisions:  
bidirectional tab-based guided navigation, a pre-training configuration  
summary that makes every automated decision visible and correctable before  
execution, and accessible result visualizations that enable domain experts  
to evaluate model outputs without statistical expertise. The primary gaps  
identified concern pipeline configurability during model development  
phases and the absence of parameter comparison functionality in the  
current version of the tool.
  
From the \gls{hci} perspective, this work illustrates that interface design  
for \gls{ml} systems is a determining factor for effective and responsible  
human-AI collaboration. The design principles identified --- sequential  
guided navigation, explicit responsibility allocation and modular  
architectural separation --- constitute a reusable pattern vocabulary for  
designing or evaluating any \gls{ml} tool intended for non-expert users.  
As future work, the most immediate priority is conducting formal usability  
studies with independent evaluators and real users,  
complemented by the incorporation of \gls{xai} techniques, the implementation  
of parameter comparison functionality and the extension toward heterogeneous  
data sources.
