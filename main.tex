\abstract{Human-Guided Machine Learning (HGML) proposes actively integrating expert knowledge into the Machine Learning (ML) lifecycle. However, no established methodology exists for evaluating HGML tools under Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence (HCAI) criteria in a systematic and reproducible manner. This paper proposes a structured evaluation methodology that operationalizes the two-dimensional HCAI framework into concrete pipeline phases, responsibility assignment criteria and a four-step evaluation procedure formalized as an HCAI-HGML Compliance Matrix. The methodology is validated through its application to InsuML, an HGML tool designed for users without AI expertise, demonstrating its practical utility.}

\section{\uppercase{Introduction}}
\label{sec:introduction}

The incorporation of \gls{ml} into non-technical professional domains poses a fundamental challenge: how can domain experts leverage the predictive potential of \gls{ml} without requiring specialized training in data science? \Gls{automl} tools ---software systems that   
automate the construction of \gls{ml} models--- have taken significant steps in this direction, but their predominant orientation toward optimizing predictive performance has relegated equally relevant aspects to the background, such as process transparency, the possibility of user intervention and the comprehensibility of results \cite{barbudo2023eight}.

In parallel, the paradigm of \gls{hcai} has gained relevance as a conceptual and evaluative framework for the design of intelligent systems that place the user at the center of the process, not as a mere recipient of results, but as an active participant with control and interpretation capabilities \cite{shneiderman2022human}. However, the practical application of \gls{hcai} criteria to concrete \gls{automl} tools remains scarce in the literature: while theoretical frameworks abound, few tools have been systematically assessed against human-centered design principles.

This scarcity, however, is not merely a matter of quantity. Even when \gls{hcai} criteria are applied, a more fundamental gap persists: there is no established methodology for doing so systematically. Existing \gls{hcai} frameworks, while conceptually robust, are formulated at a level of generality that does not translate directly into actionable evaluation instruments for the specific characteristics of \gls{hgml} systems \cite{gil2019towards}. Key questions remain unanswered: which pipeline phases should be examined? What criteria determine whether a decision genuinely belongs to the user or to the system? How can the resulting human-automation balance be characterized in a reproducible and comparable manner? Addressing these questions is a prerequisite for turning \gls{hcai} evaluation from an interpretive exercise into a principled methodological contribution. This gap is precisely what the present work seeks to address.

This work therefore makes two interrelated contributions. First, it proposes a structured methodology for evaluating \gls{hgml} tools under \gls{hcai} criteria, grounded in existing \gls{hgml} pipeline frameworks. The methodology operationalizes the \gls{hcai} framework into nine pipeline phases, explicit responsibility assignment criteria and a Compliance Matrix that yields a structured \gls{hcai} profile for any \gls{hgml} tool. And second, it validates the methodology through its application to \gls{insuml}, an \gls{hgml} tool designed for non-expert users.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Section~\ref{sec:background} reviews the relevant background on \gls{automl}, \gls{hcai} and \gls{hgml}; Section~\ref{sec:methodology} proposes the evaluation methodology; Section~\ref{sec:application} presents \gls{insuml} and its \gls{hcai} evaluation; Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the findings and their implications; and Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} summarizes the conclusions and future work.


\section{\uppercase{Background}}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{AutoML: Automation of the ML Pipeline}

\Gls{automl} tools emerged in response to the growing demand for \gls{ml} solutions and the shortage of qualified data scientists \cite{he2021automl}. Their purpose is to democratize access to \gls{ml} by automating complex tasks such as feature engineering, model selection, hyperparameter optimization and evaluation, making advanced analytics accessible to users without deep technical knowledge.

However, this predominantly automation-oriented approach frequently operates as a black box, making it difficult for users to understand decision-making processes, incorporate domain-specific knowledge or maintain meaningful control over the modeling process \cite{garouani2023unlocking}. This limitation is not minor: the lack of transparency can conceal underlying biases and prevent the human-AI collaboration necessary to obtain reliable and contextually appropriate results \cite{komala2024fair}.

The current market offers both commercial solutions ---such as Google AutoML, Azure Automated ML or Amazon SageMaker Autopilot--- and free alternatives ---including Ludwig, AutoGluon, H2O-3 or PyCaret---, designed to meet varying levels of technical expertise. However, the analysis of these tools reveals that none simultaneously maximizes human control and automation, each representing a different balance between accessibility, interpretability and technical sophistication \cite{palazuelos2024non}.

\subsection{Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence}

\Gls{hcai} represents a fundamental reorientation in the design and evaluation of intelligent systems. Unlike traditional approaches that prioritize algorithmic performance as the primary success criterion, \gls{hcai} places the user at the center of the system lifecycle, emphasizing that \gls{ai} should amplify and enhance human capabilities rather than replace them \cite{shneiderman2022human}. In the so-called third wave of \gls{ai}, \gls{hci} professionals play a central role not only in designing user experiences but also in shaping intelligent systems that are ethical, comprehensible and useful \cite{xu2019toward}.

A distinctive aspect of this paradigm is its two-dimensional conceptualization (Figure~\ref{fig:HCAI_dimensions}). Unlike the classical notion of autonomy, which assumes an inverse relationship between automation and human control, \gls{hcai} treats both dimensions as independent. This allows conceiving systems that simultaneously combine high levels of automation with meaningful human control, representing the most desirable design space for \gls{ai} tools aimed at non-expert users.

This duality is articulated around three human control criteria ---ease of use, pipeline configurability and information interpretability--- and six automation criteria ---data type detection, data cleaning, feature engineering, model training, hyperparameter optimization and model validation---, forming an evaluative framework applicable to concrete tools. Among the risks that \gls{hcai} seeks to mitigate are algorithmic bias, frequently caused by imbalanced training data, and the lack of transparency inherent to systems that operate as black boxes \cite{chen2023human}.

\subsection{Human-Guided Machine Learning}

\Gls{hgml} constitutes the practical realization of \gls{hcai} principles within the \gls{ml} domain \cite{gil2019towards}. Its fundamental premise is that the active participation of domain experts throughout the \gls{ml} pipeline is not only possible but beneficial, enriching the process with contextual knowledge that algorithms alone cannot incorporate. While \gls{automl} efficiently searches for solutions, expert input through intuitive interfaces enhances the process, resulting in more accurate, efficient and interpretable models \cite{lindauer2024position}.

Within the \gls{hgml} framework, this participation is structured around three main areas. In the data preparation phase, users can intervene in variable selection and transformation, the definition of subpopulations of interest and the management of outliers. During model development, the expert can guide the selection of algorithm families, impose constraints on parameters and influence pipeline design. Finally, in the results interpretation phase, users can request specific metrics, compare alternative configurations and analyze model sensitivity to parameter variations.

Despite its potential, existing \gls{hgml} proposals present notable limitations in practice. Although some theoretical frameworks cover specific parts of the pipeline, few offer complete software implementations accessible to users without \gls{ai} training, considerably reducing their applicability in real-world contexts \cite{gil2019towards,francia2023hamlet,lee2020human,lindauer2024position}.


\section{\uppercase{A Methodology for Evaluating HGML Tools under HCAI Criteria}}
\label{sec:methodology}

To apply the methodology, the evaluator systematically traverses the tool's
complete workflow, documents every decision point encountered by the user and
every automated action performed by the system, assesses the degree to which
each pipeline phase satisfies the human control and automation criteria, and
computes two aggregate scores that position the tool within the \gls{hcai}
two-dimensional framework. The following subsections define the evaluation
criteria, the theoretical reference framework that guides the assessment, and
the instrument used to formalize and quantify the results.

\subsection{Evaluation Dimensions and Criteria}
\label{subsec:dimensions}

\textbf{Human control dimension} is assessed through three criteria:
(i)~\textit{ease of use}, examining whether the tool can be operated by users
without \gls{ai} expertise through its interface design and interaction
patterns; (ii)~\textit{pipeline configurability}, measuring the extent to
which users can customize key decisions across pipeline phases without
requiring code-level intervention; and (iii)~\textit{information
interpretability}, evaluating whether the system communicates its decisions,
configurations and results in language and formats accessible to non-technical
users.

\textbf{Computer automation dimension} is assessed through six criteria
corresponding to the core technical tasks of the \gls{ml} pipeline:
(i)~\textit{data type detection}, examining whether the system automatically
infers the nature of each variable ---binary, number, category, text or
other--- and applies appropriate encoders without requiring explicit user
specification; (ii)~\textit{data cleaning}, evaluating the extent to which
the system autonomously handles missing values, normalizes distributions and
encodes categorical variables beyond the options exposed to the user;
(iii)~\textit{feature engineering}, assessing whether the system automatically
constructs, transforms or selects features from the raw input data without
requiring manual specification; (iv)~\textit{model training}, determining
whether the system autonomously executes the training process, including
architecture selection and optimization, without requiring technical
intervention from the user; (v)~\textit{hyperparameter optimization},
measuring the degree to which the system searches and tunes hyperparameters
within predefined or inferred ranges without user involvement; and
(vi)~\textit{model validation}, evaluating whether the system automatically
applies standard validation procedures ---such as cross-validation or hold-out
splitting--- and reports diagnostic metrics.

\subsection{Theoretical Responsibility Framework}
\label{subsec:responsibility}

The central analytical operation of the methodology is the assignment of each
decision within a pipeline phase to one of three responsibility categories:
\textbf{User} (decisions requiring domain knowledge or contextual judgment,
e.g., which variables are semantically relevant or what prediction error is
acceptable); \textbf{System} (decisions requiring technical \gls{ml}
expertise or computational search inaccessible to non-expert users, e.g.,
hyperparameter optimization or cross-validation); and \textbf{Shared}
(decisions where the user provides high-level guidance and the system executes
within those bounds, e.g., the user selects evaluation metrics and the system
computes and reports them).

\subsection{The HCAI-HGML Compliance Matrix}
\label{subsec:matrix}

The \gls{hcai}-\gls{hgml} Compliance Matrix crosses the nine pipeline phases
(rows) against the nine evaluation criteria (columns): three human control
criteria and six automation criteria. Guided by the reference in
Table~\ref{tab:balance}, the evaluator records for each cell whether the tool
\textbf{fully meets} the criterion in that phase (\checkmark),
\textbf{partially meets} it ($\sim$), or \textbf{does not meet it or it is
not applicable} (---).

Once the matrix is fully populated, two aggregate scores are computed. Let
$n = 9$ be the number of pipeline phases, $c_{HC} = 3$ the number of human
control criteria and $c_{A} = 6$ the number of automation criteria. For each
cell, a value of $1$ is assigned to \checkmark, $0.5$ to $\sim$, and $0$ to
---. The \textbf{Human Control Score} and \textbf{Automation Score} are
defined as:

\begin{equation}
    S_{HC} = \frac{1}{n \cdot c_{HC}} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j=1}^{c_{HC}} v_{ij}
    \label{eq:shc}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    S_{A} = \frac{1}{n \cdot c_{A}} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j=1}^{c_{A}} v_{ij}
    \label{eq:sa}
\end{equation}

where $v_{ij} \in \{0, 0.5, 1\}$ is the value assigned to cell $(i,j)$.
Both scores range from 0 to~1 and together determine the \gls{hcai} quadrant
of the tool under evaluation, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:quadrants}.

\begin{table*}[ht]
\caption{\gls{hcai} quadrant classification based on $S_{HC}$ and $S_{A}$.}
\label{tab:quadrants}
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lllp{6cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Quadrant} & $S_{HC}$ & $S_{A}$ & \textbf{Interpretation} \\
\midrule
Optimal (upper right)                    & $> 0.5$ & $> 0.5$ & High human control and high automation: target \gls{hcai} profile \\
Automation without control (upper left)  & $\leq 0.5$ & $> 0.5$ & Highly automated but opaque to the user \\
Control without automation (lower right) & $> 0.5$ & $\leq 0.5$ & User-driven but insufficiently automated \\
Deficient (lower left)                   & $\leq 0.5$ & $\leq 0.5$ & Neither adequate control nor automation \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table*}

Beyond quadrant classification, the \textbf{distance to the ideal point}
$(1, 1)$ provides a continuous measure of proximity to a perfect
\gls{hcai}-compliant \gls{hgml} tool:

\begin{equation}
    D = \sqrt{(1 - S_{HC})^2 + (1 - S_{A})^2}
    \label{eq:distance}
\end{equation}

$D$ ranges from $0$ (perfect compliance) to $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$ (worst
case). A tool in the optimal quadrant with a low $D$ value is close to the
ideal; a high $D$ value, even within the optimal quadrant, reveals specific
dimensions that require improvement. The individual scores $S_{HC}$ and
$S_{A}$ further indicate which dimension ---human control or automation---
constitutes the primary gap.


\section{\uppercase{Application to InsuML}}
\label{sec:application}

\subsection{Tool Overview, Design Principles and Interface}

\Gls{insuml} is a software application designed to facilitate the construction of predictive models by users without \gls{ai} expertise. Its design rests on a central premise: that domain knowledge and expert intuition are valuable assets that must be explicitly integrated into the \gls{ml} process, rather than being displaced by automation. To this end, the tool adopts the \gls{hgml} paradigm as its structuring principle, combining the power of an \gls{automl} engine with a guided \gls{gui} that makes every pipeline decision visible and controllable \cite{shneiderman2022human}.

The interface is organized through a tab-based navigation system that guides users sequentially through the complete \gls{ml} pipeline without requiring programming skills. The workflow begins with a welcome screen that contextualizes the process, followed by dedicated tabs for dataset loading, variable configuration, training parameter selection and results visualization. Figure~\ref{fig:tab-based_interface} shows the feature configuration tab, illustrating the dataset preview, variable assignment controls and missing value handling options.

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \caption{InsuML graphical user interface: the feature configuration tab showing dataset preview, variable assignment controls and missing value handling options.}
    \label{fig:tab-based_interface}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{ventana_preprocess_features.jpg}
\end{figure*}

During the configuration phase, users can remove irrelevant variables, assign input or output roles to each column, select evaluation metrics and define strategies for handling missing values. Once training is complete, results are presented through interactive graphics and confusion matrices that facilitate interpretation without requiring advanced statistical expertise. This design transforms habitually opaque technical decisions into explicit and comprehensible choices for the user, addressing one of the most frequent criticisms directed at conventional \gls{automl} systems \cite{garouani2023unlocking}.

\subsection{Architecture}

\Gls{insuml} is built on three modular layers: an Interface Layer implemented in Python using Tkinter, which manages user interaction and input validation; a Core Logic Layer that translates user selections into technical configurations and automatically generates the necessary configuration files; and an \gls{automl} Layer based on the Ludwig engine, which handles training, hyperparameter optimization and model evaluation autonomously. This separation ensures that all automated decisions can be traced and understood, contributing to bias mitigation and responsible \gls{ai} deployment \cite{chen2023human}. The modular design also allows the underlying \gls{automl} engine to be replaced without altering the user experience, ensuring flexibility and longevity.

\subsection{HCAI Evaluation: Human Control throughout the Pipeline}

The evaluation follows the four-step procedure defined in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}. The interface walkthrough was conducted through a complete traversal of \gls{insuml}'s tab-based workflow, from dataset loading to results visualization, documenting all decision points and automated actions.

\textbf{Data use.} In the variable selection phase, the user exercises direct and explicit control: they can review the complete dataset, identify irrelevant variables for their specific problem and remove them before training. This domain knowledge-based prioritization capability is precisely the type of intervention that conventional \gls{automl} systems do not contemplate. Regarding variable requirements, the user can explicitly designate which columns will act as input variables and which will be the target variable, as well as specify the problem type ---classification or regression---. Instance selection allows the user to define treatment strategies for missing values, choosing between the removal of incomplete rows or imputation by column mean, a decision with direct implications for the quality and representativeness of the resulting model.

\textbf{Model development.} Model type selection is delegated to the Ludwig engine, which automatically chooses the most appropriate architecture based on dataset characteristics and problem type. However, the user can indirectly influence this through the configuration of training parameters, such as maximum training time or preferred evaluation metrics. Solution design remains partially in the user's hands through the preprocessing decisions made in earlier phases, whose impact on the complete pipeline is visible thanks to the configuration summary the system presents before training begins.

\textbf{Model interpretation.} Once training is complete, \gls{insuml} presents results through interactive visualizations including learning curves, confusion matrices and performance metrics adapted to the task type. This set of resources facilitates the user evaluating the model from their domain perspective, identifying error patterns and making informed decisions about whether the model is suitable for their application. The possibility of reconfiguring the pipeline and retraining the model also enables an implicit comparison between alternative configurations, fostering the iterative exploration characteristic of the \gls{hgml} approach.

\subsection{HCAI Evaluation: Pipeline Automation}

The Ludwig engine autonomously manages a broad set of technical tasks that would otherwise require advanced expertise. Automatic data type detection allows the system to infer whether each variable is numerical, categorical or of another type, applying appropriate encoders and transformations without user intervention. Data cleaning, beyond the user-configurable options, includes automatic normalization mechanisms and categorical variable encoding. Model training is executed entirely in an automated fashion, including neural architecture selection, hyperparameter optimization within predefined ranges and validation using standard techniques. All of this occurs transparently for the user, who receives continuous feedback on training progress without needing to intervene in the technical details.

The analysis reveals that \gls{insuml} achieves a genuine balance between both dimensions. Decisions requiring domain knowledge ---which variables are relevant, what type of problem is being addressed, what level of error is acceptable--- fall explicitly on the user. Tasks demanding technical \gls{ml} expertise ---hyperparameter optimization, architecture selection, cross-validation--- are managed autonomously by the system. This distribution is not coincidental but the result of deliberate design decisions oriented toward placing the tool in the high automation and high human control quadrant of the \gls{hcai} framework \cite{shneiderman2022human}.

\subsection{Evaluation of Transversal Criteria}

Beyond the phase-by-phase analysis, the evaluation of the \gls{hcai} framework's transversal criteria offers a complementary view of the degree of compliance with human-centered design principles.

Regarding \textbf{ease of use}, the tab-based guided navigation, the absence of programming requirements and the progressive presentation of options significantly reduce the entry barrier for users without technical training. Automatic input validation and the comprehensible error messaging system further contribute to a robust and accessible user experience.

With respect to \textbf{pipeline configurability}, \gls{insuml} offers a medium-high level of flexibility. Users can customize key aspects of the process ---variables, metrics, imputation strategies, training time--- without needing to modify code. However, the dependency on the Ludwig engine imposes limitations on the configuration of model architectures at a low level, an aspect that represents a future improvement opportunity.

Regarding \textbf{information interpretability}, the system presents results through interactive visualizations and accessible language, avoiding the direct exposure of technical parameters to the user. The pre-training configuration summary is a particularly valuable element, as it allows the user to verify and understand exactly what process will be executed before initiating it, transforming automated decisions into transparent and verifiable options \cite{garouani2023unlocking}.


\section{\uppercase{Discussion}}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{InsuML in the AutoML and HGML Landscape}

The \gls{hcai} evaluation presented in the previous section allows \gls{insuml} to be situated within the current \gls{automl} landscape from a perspective that goes beyond predictive performance. Tools such as AutoGluon or H2O-3 achieve more competitive results on most benchmarks, but their predominant orientation toward technically proficient users, the absence of guided workflows and the limited interpretability of their interfaces place them in a design space different from that advocated by \gls{hcai} \cite{lindauer2024position}. \Gls{insuml} does not compete in the same space as these tools: it competes in the human-centered design space, where accessibility, transparency and meaningful control are the primary success criteria.

The comparison with existing \gls{hgml} proposals further reinforces this distinction. Works such as those of Gil et al. \cite{gil2019towards} and Lee et al. \cite{lee2020human} establish comprehensive theoretical frameworks covering the full \gls{ml} pipeline from a human-centered perspective, but lack software implementation and have not been evaluated under \gls{hcai} criteria. The HAMLET framework of Francia et al. \cite{francia2023hamlet}, while providing partial software support, is specifically oriented toward \gls{ai} experts, limiting its applicability to the non-technical user profile that \gls{hgml} aims to reach. Lindauer et al. \cite{lindauer2024position} explicitly call for more human-centered \gls{automl} tools, acknowledging that current proposals insufficiently address usability, interpretability and customization criteria. To the best of our knowledge, \gls{insuml} is among the few proposals that combine full pipeline coverage, operational software implementation, accessibility for non-AI users and explicit evaluation under \gls{hcai} criteria. While Xu et al. \cite{xu2026hcai} advance \gls{hcai} methodology at a systemic level, the present work complements their contribution by providing an operationalized, pipeline-specific evaluation instrument for \gls{hgml} tools.

\subsection{Prospective Transparency and Design Implications}

A concept that emerges from the evaluation of \gls{insuml} and deserves explicit articulation is what we term \textit{prospective transparency}: the capacity of a system to make its forthcoming automated behavior visible and verifiable to the user \textit{before} that behavior is executed, rather than explaining it \textit{after the fact}. This concept is related to, but distinct from, post-hoc explainability \cite{arrieta2020explainable} and algorithmic transparency \cite{diakopoulos2016accountability}: unlike these, prospective transparency operates at the interaction level rather than the model level, and is triggered before execution rather than after. To our knowledge, no prior work has explicitly defined this property as a design criterion for \gls{ml} interfaces.

In \gls{insuml}, prospective transparency is materialized through the pre-training configuration summary: a checkpoint that presents the complete set of user-selected options and system-applied defaults before training begins. This mechanism transforms the transition from user configuration to automated execution ---habitually an opaque boundary in conventional \gls{automl} systems--- into an explicit verification step. The user is not merely informed of what the system did; they are invited to confirm what the system \textit{will do}, preserving their agency at the most consequential decision point in the pipeline.

More broadly, the \gls{hcai} evaluation of \gls{insuml} yields transferable insights for the design of future \gls{ml} tools targeting non-expert users. Sequential tab-based navigation proves to be an effective interaction pattern for guiding users through complex processes without overwhelming them with simultaneous technical options. The architectural separation between the interface and core logic layers carries ethical implications beyond its technical advantages: by making the translation between user intentions and technical parameters explicit, the system reduces the risk of inadvertent perpetuation of data biases \cite{chen2023human,komala2024fair}. Together, these design principles constitute a reusable pattern vocabulary for human-centered \gls{ml} interface design.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

The evaluation presented in this work is not without limitations. Although \gls{insuml} was co-designed with non-expert users, the current work lacks formal usability studies measuring empirical metrics such as task completion time, error rates or user satisfaction with actual domain specialists performing realistic modeling tasks. This empirical validation constitutes the most immediate priority for future work, and should be conducted following established \gls{hci} research methods \cite{lazar2017research}.

Additionally, \gls{insuml}'s current explainability capabilities are limited. Incorporating \gls{xai} techniques such as \gls{shap} or \gls{lime} \cite{salih2025perspective} would considerably enrich the model interpretation phase, enabling users to understand not only global model performance but also which variables contribute to each prediction and why. Finally, experiments have been restricted to moderately sized datasets in structured tabular format; extending the tool toward larger-scale datasets and heterogeneous data sources ---text, images, time series--- would significantly broaden its real-world applicability while maintaining the human-centered design principles that articulate its development.


\section{\uppercase{Conclusions}}
\label{sec:conclusions}

This paper has presented two interrelated contributions. First, a structured methodology for evaluating \gls{hgml} tools under \gls{hcai} criteria, which operationalizes the two-dimensional \gls{hcai} framework into concrete pipeline phases, responsibility assignment criteria and a reproducible four-step expert-based heuristic evaluation procedure formalized as an \gls{hcai}-\gls{hgml} Compliance Matrix. Second, an application of this methodology to \gls{insuml}, an \gls{hgml} tool designed for users without \gls{ai} expertise, demonstrating that high automation and high human control are not opposing objectives but complementary ones when interaction design is approached with rigor and with the non-expert user as the protagonist.

The application yields two concrete findings. \Gls{insuml} achieves a genuine balance between both \gls{hcai} dimensions through deliberate design decisions: tab-based guided navigation, a pre-training configuration summary that instantiates the principle of \textit{prospective transparency}, and accessible result visualizations. This combination represents a concrete contribution to the democratization of \gls{ml}: by making every process decision visible and controllable, the tool enables domain experts to apply their contextual knowledge and prevent problematic algorithmic behaviors.

From the \gls{hci} perspective, this work illustrates that interface design for \gls{ml} systems is not a secondary concern but a determining factor for effective and responsible human-AI collaboration. As future work, the most immediate priority is conducting formal usability studies with real users \cite{lazar2017research}, complemented by the incorporation of \gls{xai} techniques and the extension toward heterogeneous data sources, maintaining human-centered design principles as the articulating axis throughout.
