\abstract{Human-Guided Machine Learning (HGML) proposes actively integrating expert knowledge into the Machine Learning lifecycle. However, no established methodology exists for evaluating HGML tools under Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence (HCAI) criteria in a systematic and reproducible manner. This paper proposes a structured evaluation methodology that operationalizes the two-dimensional HCAI framework into concrete pipeline phases, responsibility assignment criteria and a Compliance Matrix that yields a structured HCAI profile for any HGML tool. The methodology is validated through its application to InsuML, an HGML tool designed for users without AI expertise, revealing both its strengths and the precise gaps that future iterations should address.}

\section{\uppercase{Introduction}}
\label{sec:introduction}

The incorporation of \gls{ml} into non-technical professional domains poses a fundamental challenge: how can domain experts leverage the predictive potential of \gls{ml} without requiring specialized training in data science? \Gls{automl} tools ---software systems that   
automate the construction of \gls{ml} models--- have taken significant steps in this direction, but their predominant orientation toward optimizing predictive performance has relegated equally relevant aspects to the background, such as process transparency, the possibility of user intervention and the comprehensibility of results \cite{barbudo2023eight}.

In parallel, the paradigm of \gls{hcai} has gained relevance as a conceptual and evaluative framework for the design of intelligent systems that place the user at the center of the process, not as a mere recipient of results, but as an active participant with control and interpretation capabilities \cite{shneiderman2022human}. However, the practical application of \gls{hcai} criteria to concrete \gls{automl} tools remains scarce in the literature: while theoretical frameworks abound, few tools have been systematically assessed against human-centered design principles.

This scarcity, however, is not merely a matter of quantity. Even when \gls{hcai} criteria are applied, a more fundamental gap persists: there is no established methodology for doing so systematically. Existing \gls{hcai} frameworks, while conceptually robust, are formulated at a level of generality that does not translate directly into actionable evaluation instruments for the specific characteristics of \gls{hgml} systems \cite{gil2019towards}. Key questions remain unanswered: which pipeline phases should be examined? What criteria determine whether a decision genuinely belongs to the user or to the system? How can the resulting human-automation balance be characterized in a reproducible and comparable manner? Addressing these questions is a prerequisite for turning \gls{hcai} evaluation from an interpretive exercise into a principled methodological contribution. This gap is precisely what the present work seeks to address.

This work therefore makes two interrelated contributions. First, it proposes a structured methodology for evaluating \gls{hgml} tools under \gls{hcai} criteria, grounded in existing \gls{hgml} pipeline frameworks. The methodology operationalizes the \gls{hcai} framework into nine pipeline phases, explicit responsibility assignment criteria and a Compliance Matrix that yields a structured \gls{hcai} profile for any \gls{hgml} tool. And second, it validates the methodology through its application to \gls{insuml}, an \gls{hgml} tool designed for non-expert users.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Section~\ref{sec:background} reviews the relevant background on \gls{automl}, \gls{hcai} and \gls{hgml}; Section~\ref{sec:methodology} proposes the evaluation methodology; Section~\ref{sec:application} presents \gls{insuml} and its \gls{hcai} evaluation; Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the findings and their implications; and Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} summarizes the conclusions and future work.


\section{\uppercase{Background}}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{AutoML: Automation of the ML Pipeline}

\Gls{automl} tools emerged in response to the growing demand for \gls{ml} solutions and the shortage of qualified data scientists \cite{he2021automl}. Their purpose is to democratize access to \gls{ml} by automating complex tasks such as feature engineering, model selection, hyperparameter optimization and evaluation, making advanced analytics accessible to users without deep technical knowledge.

However, this predominantly automation-oriented approach frequently operates as a black box, making it difficult for users to understand decision-making processes, incorporate domain-specific knowledge or maintain meaningful control over the modeling process \cite{garouani2023unlocking}. This limitation is not minor: the lack of transparency can conceal underlying biases and prevent the human-AI collaboration necessary to obtain reliable and contextually appropriate results \cite{komala2024fair}.

The current market offers both commercial solutions ---such as Google AutoML, Azure Automated ML or Amazon SageMaker Autopilot--- and free alternatives ---including Ludwig, AutoGluon, H2O-3 or PyCaret---, designed to meet varying levels of technical expertise. However, the analysis of these tools reveals that none simultaneously maximizes human control and automation, each representing a different balance between accessibility, interpretability and technical sophistication \cite{palazuelos2024non}.

\subsection{Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence}

\Gls{hcai} represents a fundamental reorientation in the design and evaluation of intelligent systems. Unlike traditional approaches that prioritize algorithmic performance as the primary success criterion, \gls{hcai} places the user at the center of the system lifecycle, emphasizing that \gls{ai} should amplify and enhance human capabilities rather than replace them \cite{shneiderman2022human}. In the so-called third wave of \gls{ai}, \gls{hci} professionals play a central role not only in designing user experiences but also in shaping intelligent systems that are ethical, comprehensible and useful \cite{xu2019toward}.

A distinctive aspect of this paradigm is its two-dimensional conceptualization (Figure~\ref{fig:HCAI_dimensions}). Unlike the classical notion of autonomy, which assumes an inverse relationship between automation and human control, \gls{hcai} treats both dimensions as independent. This allows conceiving systems that simultaneously combine high levels of automation with meaningful human control, representing the most desirable design space for \gls{ai} tools aimed at non-expert users.

This duality is articulated around three human control criteria ---ease of use, pipeline configurability and information interpretability--- and six automation criteria ---data type detection, data cleaning, feature engineering, model training, hyperparameter optimization and model validation---, forming an evaluative framework applicable to concrete tools. Among the risks that \gls{hcai} seeks to mitigate are algorithmic bias, frequently caused by imbalanced training data, and the lack of transparency inherent to systems that operate as black boxes \cite{chen2023human}.

\subsection{Human-Guided Machine Learning}

\Gls{hgml} constitutes the practical realization of \gls{hcai} principles within the \gls{ml} domain \cite{gil2019towards}. Its fundamental premise is that the active participation of domain experts throughout the \gls{ml} pipeline is not only possible but beneficial, enriching the process with contextual knowledge that algorithms alone cannot incorporate. While \gls{automl} efficiently searches for solutions, expert input through intuitive interfaces enhances the process, resulting in more accurate, efficient and interpretable models \cite{lindauer2024position}.

Within the \gls{hgml} framework, this participation is structured around three main areas. In the data preparation phase, users can intervene in variable selection and transformation, the definition of subpopulations of interest and the management of outliers. During model development, the expert can guide the selection of algorithm families, impose constraints on parameters and influence pipeline design. Finally, in the results interpretation phase, users can request specific metrics, compare alternative configurations and analyze model sensitivity to parameter variations.

Despite its potential, existing \gls{hgml} proposals present notable limitations in practice. Although some theoretical frameworks cover specific parts of the pipeline, few offer complete software implementations accessible to users without \gls{ai} training, considerably reducing their applicability in real-world contexts \cite{gil2019towards,francia2023hamlet,lee2020human,lindauer2024position}.


\section{\uppercase{A Methodology for Evaluating HGML Tools under HCAI Criteria}}
\label{sec:methodology}

La metodología propuesta traduce el marco bidimensional abstracto de \gls{hcai} de Shneiderman~\cite{shneiderman2022human} en un procedimiento de evaluación concreto y reproducible, fundamentado en el modelo de pipeline de nueve fases de \gls{hgml} de Gil~et~al.~\cite{gil2019towards}. El resultado es un instrumento estructurado que produce dos puntuaciones agregadas que sitúan cualquier herramienta \gls{hgml} en el espacio de cuadrantes \gls{hcai}, junto con un perfil diagnóstico por fase que identifica brechas específicas y permite recomendaciones de diseño orientadas.

\subsection{Evaluation Dimensions and Criteria}
\label{subsec:dimensions}

The \gls{hcai} framework positions tools along two independent axes: human
control and computer automation \cite{shneiderman2022human}. To make these
axes actionable for evaluation, each must be decomposed into observable
criteria that can be assessed by examining the tool's interface and behavior
across pipeline phases.

\textbf{Human control} is operationalized through three criteria that together
capture the user's capacity to understand, configure and verify the system's
behavior at each stage of the pipeline. These three criteria are not arbitrary:
they correspond to the three conditions that Shneiderman identifies as
necessary for meaningful human control in \gls{ai} systems, namely that the
system be usable without specialized expertise, that it allow the user to
shape its decisions, and that it communicate those decisions
intelligibly~\cite{shneiderman2022human}.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Ease of use}: whether the tool can be operated by users
    without \gls{ai} expertise, assessed through its interface design,
    interaction patterns and the absence of programming requirements.
    \item \textit{Pipeline configurability}: the extent to which users can
    customize key decisions across pipeline phases without code-level
    intervention, reflecting the degree to which domain knowledge can be
    actively injected into the process.
    \item \textit{Information interpretability}: whether the system
    communicates its decisions, configurations and results in language and
    formats accessible to non-technical users, enabling informed judgment
    rather than blind acceptance of outputs.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Computer automation} captures the degree to which the system
autonomously executes the technical tasks of each pipeline phase. It is
decomposed into six sub-criteria, each reflecting a distinct aspect of the
automated processing that the system performs on behalf of the user:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Data type detection}: whether the system automatically
    identifies and handles the data types present in the dataset without
    requiring explicit user specification.
    \item \textit{Data cleaning}: whether the system autonomously detects
    and resolves data quality issues such as missing values, duplicates or
    inconsistent formats.
    \item \textit{Feature engineering}: whether the system automatically
    generates, transforms or selects features from the raw input data.
    \item \textit{Model training}: whether the system autonomously executes
    the training process, including architecture instantiation and
    optimization, without requiring manual intervention.
    \item \textit{Hyperparameter tuning}: whether the system automatically
    searches for optimal hyperparameter configurations within the defined
    search space.
    \item \textit{Model validation}: whether the system autonomously applies
    validation strategies such as cross-validation or hold-out evaluation
    and reports the corresponding performance estimates.
\end{itemize}

Scoring these criteria across pipeline phases requires a reference point: a
principled description of what each phase should look like in a fully
\gls{hcai}-compliant tool, specifying which decisions are expected from the
user and which from the system. Table~\ref{tab:balance} serves as the benchmark against
which any tool under evaluation is contrasted when populating the matrix.

\subsection{The HCAI-HGML Compliance Matrix}
\label{subsec:matrix}

The \gls{hcai}-\gls{hgml} Compliance Matrix is the central instrument of the
methodology. It crosses the nine pipeline phases (rows) against four evaluation
columns: the three human control criteria (\textit{ease of use},
\textit{pipeline configurability}, \textit{information interpretability}) and
a single automation score ($A_{\text{global}}$) per phase. This asymmetric
structure reflects a deliberate methodological choice: while the six
automation sub-criteria defined in Section~\ref{subsec:dimensions} provide a
complete picture of a system's automated capabilities, not all sub-criteria
are present or relevant in every pipeline phase. Collapsing them into a single
$A_{\text{global}}$ per phase avoids artificially penalizing tools for the
absence of sub-criteria that are structurally inapplicable to a given phase,
while still capturing the overall degree of automation at each stage. Each
cell represents the degree to which the tool satisfies a given criterion in a
given phase, assessed by contrasting the tool's observed behavior against the
theoretical expectation defined in Table~\ref{tab:balance}.

All four criteria are scored on a five-level ordinal scale that provides
sufficient granularity to capture meaningful differences in implementation
quality without demanding a degree of precision that a structured interface
analysis cannot reliably support:
\textbf{0.00}~(Absent --- the criterion is not implemented);
\textbf{0.25}~(Minimal --- a rudimentary attempt with severe limitations);
\textbf{0.50}~(Partial --- approximately half of the expected behavior is
present); \textbf{0.75}~(Substantial --- the criterion is largely met with
minor identifiable gaps); and \textbf{1.00}~(Complete --- the criterion is
fully satisfied).

Once the matrix is fully populated, two aggregate scores are computed. The
\textbf{Human Control Score} $S_{HC}$ is the mean of all human control
criterion scores across all phases:

\begin{equation}
    S_{HC} = \frac{1}{n \cdot c_{HC}} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j=1}^{c_{HC}} v_{ij}
    \label{eq:shc}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $n = 9$ is the number of pipeline phases, $c_{HC} = 3$ the
number of human control criteria, and $v_{ij} \in \{0, 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 1\}$
is the ordinal score assigned to cell $(i, j)$. The \textbf{Automation Score}
$S_{A}$ is the mean of all $A_{\text{global}}$ scores across phases:

\begin{equation}
    S_{A} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} A_{\text{global},i}
    \label{eq:sa}
\end{equation}

Both scores range from 0 to 1 and together determine the \gls{hcai} quadrant
of the tool under evaluation, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:quadrants}. The
quadrant classification provides a coarse-grained summary of the tool's
\gls{hcai} profile; the distance metric introduced below refines this
characterization into a continuous measure.

\begin{table*}[ht]
\caption{\gls{hcai} quadrant classification based on $S_{HC}$ and $S_{A}$.}
\label{tab:quadrants}
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lllp{6cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Quadrant} & $S_{HC}$ & $S_{A}$ & \textbf{Interpretation} \\
\midrule
Optimal (high control, high automation)        & $> 0.5$ & $> 0.5$ & High human control and high automation: target \gls{hcai} profile \\
Automated but opaque (low control, high automation)  & $\leq 0.5$ & $> 0.5$ & Highly automated but insufficiently controllable by the user \\
Controlled but manual (high control, low automation) & $> 0.5$ & $\leq 0.5$ & User-driven but insufficiently automated \\
Deficient (low control, low automation)        & $\leq 0.5$ & $\leq 0.5$ & Neither adequate control nor automation \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table*}

The \textbf{distance to the ideal point} $(1, 1)$ provides a continuous
measure of proximity to a fully \gls{hcai}-compliant \gls{hgml} tool:

\begin{equation}
    D = \sqrt{(1 - S_{HC})^2 + (1 - S_{A})^2}
    \label{eq:distance}
\end{equation}

\noindent $D$ ranges from $0$ (perfect compliance) to $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$
(worst case). A tool in the optimal quadrant with a low $D$ value is close to
the ideal; a high $D$ value, even within the optimal quadrant, signals that
one or both dimensions require improvement. The column scores for each human
control criterion and the row scores for each pipeline phase further localize
where the primary gaps lie, enabling targeted design recommendations rather
than a single summary judgment.

Beyond the aggregate scores, the matrix is also sensitive to specific
interaction design patterns that have direct implications for scoring. A
particularly relevant one is what we term \textit{Pre-execution Review}: the
property of a system that presents the complete set of user-selected options
and system-applied defaults \textit{before} automated execution begins,
enabling verification and correction prior to commitment. Unlike post-hoc
explainability mechanisms, which account for decisions already made,
Pre-execution Review operates at the boundary between user configuration and
automated execution, transforming an habitually opaque transition into an
explicit, inspectable checkpoint. This property has direct scoring implications:
tools that implement it receive higher scores on information interpretability
across all pipeline phases, as they afford non-expert users a meaningful
opportunity to exercise informed control before the system acts.


\section{\uppercase{Application to InsuML}}
\label{sec:application}

\subsection{Tool Overview and Architecture}

\Gls{insuml} is a software application designed to facilitate the construction
of predictive models by users without \gls{ai} expertise. Its design rests on
a central premise: that domain knowledge and expert intuition are valuable
assets that must be explicitly integrated into the \gls{ml} process, rather
than being displaced by automation. To this end, the tool adopts the \gls{hgml}
paradigm as its structuring principle, combining the power of an \gls{automl}
engine with a guided \gls{gui} that makes every pipeline decision visible and
controllable \cite{shneiderman2022human}.

The interface is organized through a tab-based navigation system that guides
users sequentially through the complete \gls{ml} pipeline without requiring
programming skills. The workflow begins with a welcome screen that
contextualizes the process, followed by dedicated tabs for dataset loading,
variable configuration, training parameter selection and results visualization.
Figure~\ref{fig:tab-based_interface} shows the feature configuration tab,
illustrating the dataset preview, variable assignment controls and missing
value handling options. Once training is complete, results are presented
through interactive graphics and confusion matrices that facilitate
interpretation without requiring advanced statistical expertise.

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \caption{InsuML graphical user interface: the feature configuration tab
    showing dataset preview, variable assignment controls and missing value
    handling options.}
    \label{fig:tab-based_interface}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{ventana_preprocess_features.jpg}
\end{figure*}

\Gls{insuml} is built on three modular layers: an Interface Layer implemented
in Python using Tkinter, which manages user interaction and input validation;
a Core Logic Layer that translates user selections into technical configurations
and automatically generates the necessary configuration files; and an
\gls{automl} Layer based on the Ludwig engine, which handles training,
hyperparameter optimization and model evaluation autonomously. This separation
ensures that all automated decisions can be traced and understood, contributing
to bias mitigation and responsible \gls{ai} deployment \cite{chen2023human}.
The modular design also allows the underlying \gls{automl} engine to be
replaced without altering the user experience, ensuring flexibility and
longevity.

contrasting \gls{insuml}'s observed behavior at each pipeline phase against the
theoretical responsibility distribution of Table~\ref{tab:balance}.
Table~\ref{tab:compliance_matrix} presents the resulting \gls{hcai}-\gls{hgml}
Compliance Matrix, with each cell scored on the five-level ordinal scale
defined in Section~\ref{subsec:matrix}.

Ease of use reaches a uniform score of 1.00 across all phases, reflecting the
consistent absence of programming requirements, the progressive disclosure of
options and the robustness of input validation throughout the workflow. Pipeline
configurability presents the most significant variation: data use phases
---variable selection, variable requirements and model assessment--- score 1.00,
as users can directly prioritize variables, assign roles, select metrics and
define missing value strategies based on domain knowledge. Model development
phases score substantially lower (0.25--0.50), as the Ludwig engine's
architectural choices are not directly accessible to the user, who can only
influence them indirectly through training parameter configuration. Information
interpretability is high across data use and model interpretation phases
(0.75--1.00), supported by interactive visualizations, accessible language and
the pre-training configuration summary that instantiates the \textit{Pre-execution
Review} pattern defined in Section~\ref{subsec:matrix}. The model selection
phase constitutes the primary exception (0.50), as the opacity of automatic
architecture selection limits the user's capacity to understand and anticipate
the system's choices. Across the automation dimension, the Ludwig engine
achieves complete automation ($A_{\text{global}} = 1.00$) in the variable
requirements, model selection and parameter settings phases; the remaining
phases score 0.75, reflecting substantial but not exhaustive automation where
certain preprocessing decisions remain outside the engine's default scope.

Applying Equations~\ref{eq:shc} and~\ref{eq:sa}, the aggregate scores are
$S_{HC} = 0.84$ and $S_{A} = 0.83$, placing \gls{insuml} firmly in the
\textbf{Optimal quadrant} of the \gls{hcai} framework, with a distance to the
ideal point of $D = 0.23$.


\section{\uppercase{Discussion}}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{InsuML in the AutoML and HGML Landscape}

The \gls{hcai} evaluation presented in the previous section allows
\gls{insuml} to be situated within the current \gls{automl} landscape from a
perspective that goes beyond predictive performance. Tools such as AutoGluon
or H2O-3 achieve more competitive results on most benchmarks, but their
predominant orientation toward technically proficient users, the absence of
guided workflows and the limited interpretability of their interfaces place
them in a design space different from that advocated by \gls{hcai}
\cite{lindauer2024position}. \Gls{insuml} does not compete in the same space
as these tools: it competes in the human-centered design space, where
accessibility, transparency and meaningful control are the primary success
criteria.

The comparison with existing \gls{hgml} proposals further reinforces this
distinction. Works such as those of Gil et al. \cite{gil2019towards} and Lee
et al. \cite{lee2020human} establish comprehensive theoretical frameworks
covering the full \gls{ml} pipeline from a human-centered perspective, but
lack software implementation and have not been evaluated under \gls{hcai}
criteria. The HAMLET framework of Francia et al. \cite{francia2023hamlet},
while providing partial software support, is specifically oriented toward
\gls{ai} experts, limiting its applicability to the non-technical user profile
that \gls{hgml} aims to reach. Lindauer et al. \cite{lindauer2024position}
explicitly call for more human-centered \gls{automl} tools, acknowledging that
current proposals insufficiently address usability, interpretability and
customization criteria. To the best of our knowledge, \gls{insuml} is among
the few proposals that combine full pipeline coverage, operational software
implementation, accessibility for non-AI users and explicit evaluation under
\gls{hcai} criteria. While Xu et al. \cite{xu2026hcai} advance \gls{hcai}
methodology at a systemic level, the present work complements their
contribution by providing an operationalized, pipeline-specific evaluation
instrument for \gls{hgml} tools.

\subsection{Pre-execution Review as a Design Principle}

A concept that emerges from the evaluation of \gls{insuml} and deserves
explicit articulation as a transferable design principle is \textit{Pre-execution
Review}: the capacity of a system to make its forthcoming automated behavior
visible and verifiable to the user \textit{before} that behavior is executed,
rather than explaining it \textit{after the fact}. This concept is related to,
but distinct from, post-hoc explainability \cite{arrieta2020explainable} and
algorithmic transparency \cite{diakopoulos2016accountability}: unlike these,
Pre-execution Review operates at the interaction level rather than the model
level, and is triggered before execution rather than after. To our knowledge,
no prior work has explicitly defined this property as a design criterion for
\gls{ml} interfaces.

In \gls{insuml}, Pre-execution Review is materialized through the pre-training
configuration summary: a checkpoint that presents the complete set of
user-selected options and system-applied defaults before training begins. This
mechanism transforms the transition from user configuration to automated
execution ---habitually an opaque boundary in conventional \gls{automl}
systems--- into an explicit verification step. The user is not merely informed
of what the system did; they are invited to confirm what the system
\textit{will do}, preserving their agency at the most consequential decision
point in the pipeline.

More broadly, the \gls{hcai} evaluation of \gls{insuml} yields transferable
insights for the design of future \gls{ml} tools targeting non-expert users.
Sequential tab-based navigation proves to be an effective interaction pattern
for guiding users through complex processes without overwhelming them with
simultaneous technical options. The architectural separation between the
interface and core logic layers carries ethical implications beyond its
technical advantages: by making the translation between user intentions and
technical parameters explicit, the system reduces the risk of inadvertent
perpetuation of data biases \cite{chen2023human,komala2024fair}. Together,
Pre-execution Review, tab-based guided navigation and transparent
responsibility allocation constitute a reusable pattern vocabulary for
human-centered \gls{ml} interface design that \gls{hci} practitioners can
apply independently of the underlying \gls{automl} engine.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

The evaluation presented in this work is not without limitations. The
Compliance Matrix scores are assigned through structured interface analysis, and although the methodology provides explicit criteria and a
theoretical reference for each cell, the ordinal scale retains an inherent
degree of interpretive judgment. Formal inter-rater reliability studies,
involving independent evaluators applying the matrix to the same tool, would
strengthen the instrument's reproducibility and constitute a natural next step
for its validation.

Additionally, although \gls{insuml} was co-designed with non-expert users,
the current work lacks formal usability studies measuring empirical metrics
such as task completion time, error rates or user satisfaction with actual
domain specialists performing realistic modeling tasks. This empirical
validation constitutes the most immediate priority for future work, and should
be conducted following established \gls{hci} research methods
\cite{lazar2017research}.

Finally, \gls{insuml}'s current explainability capabilities are limited.
Incorporating \gls{xai} techniques such as \gls{shap} or \gls{lime}
\cite{salih2025perspective} would considerably enrich the model interpretation
phase, enabling users to understand not only global model performance but also
which variables contribute to each prediction and why. Extending the tool
toward larger-scale datasets and heterogeneous data sources ---text, images,
time series--- would further broaden its real-world applicability while
maintaining the human-centered design principles that articulate its
development.


\section{\uppercase{Conclusions}}
\label{sec:conclusions}

This paper has presented two interrelated contributions. First, a structured
methodology for evaluating \gls{hgml} tools under \gls{hcai} criteria, which
operationalizes the two-dimensional \gls{hcai} framework into concrete pipeline
phases, a theoretical responsibility distribution framework and a reproducible
expert-based evaluation procedure formalized as an \gls{hcai}-\gls{hgml}
Compliance Matrix with aggregate scores $S_{HC}$, $S_{A}$ and distance metric
$D$. Second, an application of this methodology to \gls{insuml}, an \gls{hgml}
tool designed for users without \gls{ai} expertise, yielding $S_{HC} = 0.84$,
$S_{A} = 0.83$ and $D = 0.23$, and demonstrating that high automation and high
human control are not opposing objectives but complementary ones when
interaction design is approached with rigor and with the non-expert user as
the protagonist.

The application demonstrates that \gls{insuml} achieves a genuine balance
between both \gls{hcai} dimensions through deliberate design decisions:
tab-based guided navigation, a pre-training configuration summary that
instantiates the principle of \textit{Pre-execution Review}, and accessible
result visualizations that enable domain experts to evaluate model outputs
without statistical expertise.

From the \gls{hci} perspective, this work illustrates that interface design
for \gls{ml} systems is not a secondary concern but a determining factor for
effective and responsible human-AI collaboration. The design principles
identified ---Pre-execution Review, tab-based guided navigation and explicit
responsibility allocation--- constitute a reusable pattern vocabulary that
\gls{hci} practitioners can apply when designing or evaluating any \gls{ml}
tool intended for non-expert users. As future work, the most immediate
priority is conducting formal usability studies with real users
\cite{lazar2017research}, complemented by the incorporation of \gls{xai}
techniques and the extension toward heterogeneous data sources, maintaining
human-centered design principles as the articulating axis throughout.
